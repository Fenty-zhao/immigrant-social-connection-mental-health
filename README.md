# Online Social Connection and Immigrant Mental Health

**Testing the Compensatory Role of Social Media Use Before and After COVID-19**

*MSc Social Research Methods and Statistics dissertation · University of Manchester · 2026*

## Research statement

Can social media compensate for weaker offline friendships in supporting immigrants' mental health? I examined this question using two waves of the [Understanding Society (UKHLS)](https://www.understandingsociety.ac.uk/) panel survey. Rather than estimating only a direct relationship between social media use and mental health, the analysis tested **moderation**: whether the association between friendship quality and psychological distress differs by social media use.

The dissertation used **individual fixed-effects regression**, interaction terms, and extended specifications to distinguish within-person associations from stable differences between individuals.

---

## Research at a glance

| | Study design |
| --- | --- |
| **Population** | UK residents born outside the UK |
| **Dataset** | Understanding Society, Wave 11 (2019–2020) and Wave 14 (2022–2023) |
| **Initial balanced sample** | 1,592 respondents present in both waves |
| **Main model sample** | 1,408 individuals; 2,511 person-wave observations |
| **Outcome** | GHQ-12 psychological distress score (higher = worse mental health) |
| **Key predictors** | Friendship quality; frequency of social media viewing and posting |
| **Primary method** | Individual and wave fixed-effects models; friendship × social-media interaction |
| **Extended analyses** | Cluster-robust inference, separate viewing/posting measures, time-varying controls, COVID-period interaction, nonlinear specifications |

## Key findings

<details>
<summary><strong>1. Friendship quality was associated with psychological wellbeing.</strong></summary>

The submitted baseline model reported **β = −1.225, p < 0.001** for friendship quality. A higher friendship-quality score was associated with **lower GHQ-12 distress** within the model. This is an association, not an experimentally identified effect.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>2. The social-media compensation hypothesis was not statistically supported.</strong></summary>

The central friendship-quality × social-media interaction was **β = −0.131, p = 0.487** in M2; with individual cluster-robust standard errors (M2r), **p = 0.555**. The study did **not** find convincing statistical evidence that social media use changes the friendship-quality/mental-health association.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>3. No statistically clear change in moderation across the COVID comparison.</strong></summary>

The extended three-way interaction with wave was **β = −0.170, p = 0.560** (M4). The pre/post comparison describes two survey periods; it does not isolate the causal effect of the pandemic.

</details>

**The non-significant moderation finding is a central result**, not a result to hide: frequency of looking at or posting on social media does not establish whether someone's online connections are supportive or meaningful.
